@title Compute Like Water
@subtitle Electricity, literacy, and the long project of turning guarded crafts into things that flow
@speaker A talk in twelve slides · c. 30 minutes
@duration 30:00

=== slide
@time 0:00
@heading Compute Like Water
@image https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Earth%27s%20City%20Lights%20by%20DMSP%2C%201994-1995%20(large).jpg?width=1200
@caption Earth's city lights, DMSP composite, NASA/NOAA.

Thank you. I want to start with the name of this talk, which I stole from an are.na channel called *compute-like-water* [ref:arena-compute-like-water] — a collection built around a single, quietly radical idea: that computation could someday be like water. Not like a product. Not like a device you buy or an account you hold. Like *water* — something a civilization plumbs into every building, prices at cost, and then forgets about, because forgetting about it is the whole point. Nobody in this room thought about the municipal water system this morning while brushing their teeth. That thoughtlessness is the highest achievement infrastructure can attain.

Look at this picture. This is the Earth at night [ref:earth-lights] — every point of light is somewhere that a very specific political project succeeded, and every dark region is somewhere it hasn't yet. I want to convince you over the next half hour that this picture is not a map of wealth, or geography, or even population. It is a map of *decisions* — and that we have made this exact kind of decision twice before, once for the written word and once for electric current, and that we are standing, right now, in the early scribal age of a third such technology.

So this talk is a braid of three histories: literacy, electricity, and computing. The pattern repeats three times, and by the end I want the pattern to be so familiar that you can see exactly where we are in it — and what comes next.

@time 2:30
@heading The Priesthood of the Word
@image https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Escribano.jpg?width=800
@caption Jean Miélot, scribe to the Duke of Burgundy, in his scriptorium, c. 1450s.

Here is the first age of the pattern. This is Jean Miélot [ref:scribe-mielot], scribe, translator, and secretary to Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, painted in his scriptorium around the 1450s. Look at his workstation — the slanted desk, the copy stand, the specialized tools. This man is not "a person who can read." He is a *professional intercessor between humanity and the written word*. For thousands of years, that was what literacy was: a craft monopoly held by scribes, priests, notaries, and the merchants who could hire them [ref:literacy-history].

And I want to be careful here, because the monopoly wasn't a conspiracy — it was an economy. Manuscripts were staggeringly expensive. Training took years. Of course the skill pooled around temples, courts, and counting houses; of course the people who held it became a caste. But notice what the caste's existence *did*: if all writing passes through a scribe, then contracts, scripture, law, and memory itself pass through him too. To read was, quite literally, to rule. The people who could not read did not experience this as oppression, mostly. They experienced it as *the way the world was*. You went to the man at the desk, the way you'd go to a well in a land without pipes.

And the guarding wasn't only passive economics; it was actively maintained. Guilds regulated who could train. The Church controlled which texts were copied, and in which language — Latin, pointedly, not the tongue anyone's mother spoke. When vernacular translation projects appeared, they were treated not as literacy programs but as *heresies*, because everyone understood, correctly, that the monopoly on the word was a monopoly on meaning. Businessmen behaved the same way with double-entry bookkeeping — a literacy-within-a-literacy, taught master to apprentice, guarded like a trade secret, because whoever could read the ledger owned the trade.

Hold that image: a technology of enormous general value, delivered exclusively through a priesthood, experienced by everyone else as a fact of nature. That's stage one, and every technology in this talk starts there.

=== slide
@time 5:00
@heading The Word Overflows
@image https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/PrintMus%20038.jpg?width=800
@caption Working replica of Gutenberg's press, International Printing Museum.

Then the vessel breaks. Around the 1440s, Gutenberg's press [ref:gutenberg-press] collapses the price of the written word by orders of magnitude. And here's the subtlety everyone gets wrong: the press did not teach a single person to read. On the day it was invented, literacy rates did not move. What the press did was make mass literacy *economically thinkable*. It converted reading from a craft justification — "why learn? you'll never own a book" — into a standing invitation.

And then — this is the crucial part — the invitation still had to be *accepted by politics*. It took the Reformation insisting ordinary people read scripture themselves; it took pamphlets and broadsides making print a public commons before it was a private possession; it took, ultimately, centuries of fighting for compulsory public schooling — a deliberate, expensive, tax-funded campaign to mass-produce readers [ref:literacy-history]. The press made it possible. The school made it real. And by 1948, the arc completes: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights writes education into the birthright of every human being [ref:udhr-education]. A skill that had been a priesthood for five thousand years became, in principle, a *right* — in about five hundred.

So the pattern has three stages, not two. Priesthood, then press, then *campaign*. Technology cuts the rope; only politics hands out what was behind it. Keep all three stages in mind, because electricity is about to run the same program — faster.

@time 8:00
@heading Electricity's Scribal Age
@image https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/PearlStreetStation.jpg?width=1000
@caption Pearl Street Station, 255–257 Pearl Street, Manhattan — first commercial central power station in America, 1882.

Electricity arrives in New York exactly the way writing arrived in Sumer: as a priestly craft performed for the powerful. December 1880: Charles Brush's arc lamps go up along Broadway [ref:brush-broadway], between 14th and 26th Streets — the original "Great White Way" — as a *spectacle*, paid for by merchants who want their windows glowing. September 4th, 1882: Edison throws the switch at Pearl Street Station [ref:pearl-street], the first commercial central power station in the country — this building, one square mile of service area, eighty-two customers.

And *who* were the eighty-two? Banks and newspapers. The offices of the New York Times. The firm of Drexel, Morgan and Company [ref:ieee-pearl]. J.P. Morgan himself, Edison's financier, wires his Madison Avenue mansion with its own basement dynamo — one of the first electrically lit private homes in the city — and it terrifies the neighbors and occasionally sets his desk on fire. Electric light in 1882 is precisely what an illuminated manuscript was in 1382: a bespoke luxury that demonstrates its owner commands scarce, learned labor. There is even a priesthood — the new caste of electrical engineers, men who alone can conjure and tame the current.

Notice also what electricity did *not* do in 1882: it did not go to tenements, and nobody thought that strange. The dark neighborhoods experienced darkness as the way the world was. Stage one, again, running on schedule.

=== slide
@time 10:30
@heading Who Owns the Streets?
@quote The corporation's wires cross the public's right of way — and therefore the public has standing to set terms.
@attribution The founding syllogism of municipal electricity rights

But electricity's Gutenberg moment comes fast — alternating current means the dynamo no longer has to live within a mile of the lamp — and with it comes the political fight, and New York City is the arena. Follow the sequence. 1888: the Great Blizzard [ref:blizzard-1888] tears down the insane jungle of overhead wires above Broadway, killing linemen and pedestrians, and the city forces the wires underground — establishing the founding principle on this slide: the city owns the streets, so the public may set terms for the current that crosses them. 1905: William Randolph Hearst runs for mayor at the head of the Municipal Ownership League [ref:hearst-1905] — city-owned gas, electricity, and transit — and comes within a few thousand votes of City Hall, amid credible claims that Tammany stole the count. Municipal ownership of electricity was not a fringe idea; it nearly ran New York.

Meanwhile the private side consolidates into what becomes, by 1936, Consolidated Edison [ref:con-edison] — and electricity settles into a curious in-between state I want to name precisely: the *common luxury*. By 1910, a New Yorker might work under electric light, ride an electric subway, shop in an electric store, study in an electric school — and go home to kerosene. Like print before mass schooling, electricity filled *public* space first. Everyone could visit it; few could own it.

And then 1931: Governor Franklin Roosevelt signs the Power Authority Act, creating what's now the New York Power Authority [ref:nypa] — public hydropower as a public trust, explicitly designed as a "yardstick" to discipline private rates. FDR's principle was blunt: the rivers belong to the people, so their power should reach the people at cost. That institution still runs — it powers this city's subways, public schools, public housing, and street lights today. Remember NYPA. It comes back at the end of this talk.

@time 13:30
@heading The Campaign: Rural Electrification
@image https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Light%20-%20Rural%20electrification%20administration%20-%20Beall.%20LCCN2010646236.jpg?width=800
@caption "Light." Lester Beall, for the Rural Electrification Administration, c. 1937.

Now stage three — the campaign. Because in 1935, fifty years after Pearl Street, the market had delivered its verdict on rural America, and the verdict was: *not profitable*. Roughly nine farms in ten had no electricity [ref:rea-act]. Farm families hauled every gallon of water by hand — think about what that means for one day of laundry — lit kerosene, and watched the towns glow on the horizon. The private utilities would not build the lines, or demanded farmers pay construction costs themselves. The market had found its natural stopping point, exactly where the profit stopped.

So the public went past it. Executive Order 7037, May 11th, 1935, creates the Rural Electrification Administration; the Rural Electrification Act makes it permanent a year later [ref:rea-act]. And look at this poster — this is Lester Beall's "Light" [ref:beall-light], from the poster series he made for the REA, now in MoMA and the Library of Congress. Understand what this object *is*: it is a literacy primer. Its audience is people who have never used electricity, and its job is to teach desire — light, radio, running water, a motor. The REA had to run an *advertising campaign for the twentieth century itself*. There is period film of this drive — a government picture called simply "Rural Electrification," preserved at the Internet Archive [ref:rural-film] — and it has the same evangelical cadence. This is what the acceptance of Gutenberg's invitation looks like when it's deliberate.

@time 16:00
@heading Neighbor-Experts
@image https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/A%20Rural%20Electrification%20Administration%20cooperative%20lineman%20at%20work%20in%20Hayti%2C%20Missouri.jpg?width=1000
@caption An REA cooperative lineman, Hayti, Missouri, early 1940s.

And here is the REA's structural genius, the thing that makes it more than just a subsidy. The government did not, mostly, pay utilities to extend their grids. It lent money, at low interest, to *farmer-owned cooperatives* [ref:rea-act] — to the neighbors themselves, incorporated. The man on this pole [ref:rea-lineman] is not an employee of a distant company; he's a member of a co-op stringing line to his own community. The incumbents fought it — they built "spite lines" to skim the densest customers and break co-op territories, they called it socialism — and the co-ops built anyway. Mid-1930s: one farm in ten has power. By 1953: more than nine in ten. Eighteen years, from priesthood to birthright, across the largest countryside on earth.

I want to sit on this for a second, because it's the load-bearing lesson of the whole talk: **the last mile of a civilizational technology is never reached by diffusion. It is reached by deputizing the people who live there.** The expertise had to stop being a priesthood not just in who *consumed* it but in who *delivered* it. The REA didn't send missionaries from the city; it minted linemen out of farmers. Hold that thought while we cross the Pacific.

@time 18:00
@heading Barefoot
@image https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Chinese%20barefoot%20doctor%20performing%20acupuncture.jpg?width=800
@caption A barefoot doctor treats a production-brigade worker, rural China.

Because the same shape of solution appears, independently, in revolutionary China — in medicine. Faced with a countryside that credentialed physicians would never reach, China trained over a million villagers — farmers with months, not years, of medical education — as "barefoot doctors" [ref:barefoot-doctor], delivering basic care, vaccination, sanitation, first aid, in the villages they already lived in. From 1968 it was national policy. They were not worse doctors. They were a *different shape* of doctor — native to the place, adequate to the common case, connected to the rare one.

Maggie Appleton, in an essay I'd assign to everyone here, "Home-Cooked Software and Barefoot Developers" [ref:appleton-home-cooked], picks up this figure and aims it at our own field. Her argument: the software industry only builds for markets large enough to feed venture returns — which means the countless small, local, weird needs of families, clubs, congregations, small shops, get nothing. They are rural America in 1934. And she proposes that the fix looks like the barefoot doctor and — I'd add — exactly like the REA co-op lineman: mint the practitioners *from the population being served*. The neighbor-expert, produced deliberately and at scale, because waiting for the professional market to reach the periphery means waiting forever.

The barefoot doctor, the co-op lineman, the schoolteacher of the common school movement — same figure, three costumes. Now: why does software need this figure at all? Because computing is still in its priesthood stage. Let me show you the priesthood from the inside.

@time 20:30
@heading The Computing Priesthood
@quote The computer revolution hasn't happened yet.
@attribution Alan Kay, OOPSLA keynote, 1997

In 1977, Alan Kay and Adele Goldberg published "Personal Dynamic Media" [ref:kay-goldberg-pdm] — the Dynabook paper — which imagined the computer not as a machine for institutions but as a *medium*, like paper: something every child would read and, crucially, *write*. Twenty years later, Kay stood up at OOPSLA and told a room full of the profession that the computer revolution hadn't happened yet [ref:kay-revolution]. His analogy was exactly ours: we are in computing's *incunabula* — the first decades after Gutenberg, when printed books still imitated manuscripts, because nobody yet knew what the press was actually *for*. We got personal computers, he said, the way the 1500s got printed indulgences: the old order, accelerated. Billions of people now *consume* through screens — and the ability to *write* the medium, to make the machine do a new thing, sits with a caste as small, as specialized, and as economically fortified as Miélot's guild ever was.

Bret Victor made the same argument historically in "The Future of Programming" [ref:victor-future] — delivered, as a bit, in the costume of 1973, showing how the humane, accessible visions of computing's founders were not disproven but simply *abandoned*, and how a field hardens into dogma its initiates mistake for nature. Sound familiar? "The way the world is." The dark tenements of 1882. The unlettered village of 1440. Every priesthood's power looks like physics from the outside.

And we even repeat the "common luxury" stage: compute today is like electricity in 1910 — ubiquitous in public, in your pocket, at work, at school — while genuine agency over it, the *literacy*, remains a craft possession. We have the priesthood; we have had the press for fifty years. What we have never had is the campaign.

@time 23:30
@heading Rooms Where Compute Flows
@quote A computer in every home — but the home computer was the wrong dream. The right dream is computing in the walls, like light from the ceiling: communal, physical, ownable by the room.
@attribution After Dynamicland

What would the campaign even be *for*? Here the are.na channel [ref:arena-compute-like-water] that named this talk earns its keep, because "compute like water" specifies the goal in a way "learn to code" never did. Go look at Bret Victor's Dynamicland [ref:dynamicland] — a nonprofit research space in Oakland where computation lives *in the room*: programs are pieces of paper on tables, visible to everyone, editable by everyone, running in a shared physical space maintained like a civic building. No screens hoarding the medium into private rectangles. Compute as a property of a *place* — the way light is a property of a room, the way water is a property of a building — communal, inspectable, repairable.

That is what water-like computing means, and notice it is *not* the same as "everyone becomes a professional programmer" — just as mass literacy never meant everyone becomes a scrivener. Mass literacy meant: reading and writing adequate to your own life — your letters, your ledger, your prayers, your politics. Computational literacy on the Dynamicland-Appleton axis means: the ordinary person can see, question, adjust, and compose the computation that runs their own home, shop, classroom, block. The common case, handled by the resident. The rare case, escalated to the specialist. Barefoot computing.

There is a small, stubborn lineage of projects working at exactly this — communal, physical, locally owned computing; some of them literally call the genre *folk computing*, which is the right name: computation as folk practice, like folk medicine and folk music, made and maintained by the people who use it, in the place where they use it. They are tiny. So were the first farmer co-ops that petitioned Washington in 1934. The point of a demonstration project is not its scale; it's that it makes the alternative *legible* — it converts "the way the world is" back into a choice.

And the delivery mechanism won't be an app store, for the same reason rural electrification wasn't a product Con Edison sold. It will look institutional: schools, libraries, co-ops — boring, load-bearing, public. The REA didn't ask farmers to individually purchase self-reliance; it built a durable structure they could *join*. When people say computational literacy is coming because the tools keep getting friendlier, remember that the price of books fell for four hundred years and mass literacy still waited for the common school. Friendlier tools are the press. They are necessary. They are not the campaign.

@time 25:30
@heading The Blip — Universality Is Not a Ratchet
@quote 685 million people without electricity in 2022 — ten million more than the year before. The first reversal in decades.
@attribution World Bank / IEA, SDG7 tracking

Now the uncomfortable part, because I've been telling a progress story and progress stories are how civilizations fall asleep. Notice what the finished campaigns did to our perception: in the rich world, electricity achieved water-status so completely that we ceased to see it as an achievement at all. It became a *civilization proxy* — one of that braid of quiet indicators, with literacy, plumbing, and the survivability of common disease, that we use to define "developed" precisely because we assume they only move in one direction. Here is the present tense. In 2022, for the first time in decades, the number of human beings without electricity *rose* — to about 685 million, ten million more than the year before, over eighty percent of them in sub-Saharan Africa [ref:worldbank-2024]. The IEA called it flatly: the first global reversal of the electrification project in living memory [ref:iea-2022]. Literacy is stalling in the same motion: the global *rate* still inches up on paper — 86 to 88 percent over the last decade — but the UN's own accounting shows about 740 million adults who cannot read, two-thirds women, and in sub-Saharan Africa the *absolute number* of illiterate adults is growing, nearly 200 million rising toward 225, as population outruns schooling; post-pandemic, an estimated seven in ten children in low- and middle-income countries couldn't read a simple sentence at age ten [ref:unesco-literacy].

And in the war zones the proxies fall *in pairs* — grid, then water pumping, then vaccine refrigeration, then schooling — because they were never independent; they hold each other up. Even New York keeps a body-memory of this: July 1977, one lightning strike, twenty-five hours, a thousand fires [ref:blackout-1977]. The lights came back because institutions existed whose *job* was to bring them back. That's the entire difference between a blip and a collapse — not the outage; the institution behind it. Universality is not a ratchet. It is maintenance — political maintenance — and it can be deferred until it fails.

@time 28:00
@heading The Next Electrification
@quote The water power of the State should belong to all the people.
@attribution Franklin D. Roosevelt, as Governor of New York

So let me land the braid. In 2023, New York State — after a four-year organizing campaign — passed the Build Public Renewables Act [ref:bpra], ordering NYPA, the very institution FDR chartered in 1931 [ref:nypa], to plan, build, own, and operate renewable generation, with bill credits for low-income customers and union labor on every project. The REA's logic, refiled under a new docket, in the same city where the eighty-two customers of Pearl Street once defined who deserved light. The pattern is not ancient history. It ran *last year*. It is available.

Here's the whole talk in four sentences. Every civilizational technology begins as a priesthood, and the priesthood always looks like physics. A press — Gutenberg's, Tesla's, the microprocessor — breaks the economics but never, by itself, hands anything to anyone. What hands it over is a campaign: the common school, the REA co-op, the barefoot doctor — always built on neighbor-experts, always fought by incumbents, always cheaper than everyone claimed and more transformative than anyone promised. And the campaign is never finished — it is maintained, or it reverses, and it is reversing in front of us right now, in electricity and literacy at once.

Computing has had its priesthood for seventy years and its press for fifty. It has never had its REA. That's not a lament — it's a *to-do item*, and Appleton's barefoot developers [ref:appleton-home-cooked] and Dynamicland's communal rooms [ref:dynamicland] are the first line-crews already up the poles. The channel this talk is named for [ref:arena-compute-like-water] is, in its quiet way, a set of blueprints. Compute like water. Light like literacy. The rope is always waiting to be re-hung — and the history of New York's electricity is the history of people repeatedly deciding to cut it.

Thank you.
