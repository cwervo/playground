# What these tests actually said

Plain-language version. Every claim here has a technical counterpart in
[`02-full-fat.md`](02-full-fat.md), section for section.

**This is not medical advice and not a diagnosis.** It is a reading of five
documents from one clinic visit, done carefully, to make a conversation with a
doctor go better.

---

## The five documents

All five are from the same day, **18 August 2026**, at one practice in New York.
They were scanned out of order, and the big one had its pages shuffled.

| What it is | Pages | Who made it |
|---|---|---|
| Evoke Comprehensive Report | 13 | Evoke Neuroscience |
| Evoke Summary Report | 1 | Evoke Neuroscience |
| Brain Insights Report | 3 | Firefly Neuroscience |
| 12-lead heart tracing (ECG) | 1 | Midmark, machine-read |
| Allergy skin-prick panel, 60 sites | 1 | Stallergenes Greer |

Between them they measure five different things about one person on one
afternoon: brainwaves, brain response timing, heart rhythm, button-press
performance, and immune reactivity.

---

## The headline

**The test was ordered to look for ADHD. It did not find it, and it was fairly
confident about that** — the vendor's own screen came back negative at 90%
confidence.

That is a real answer, not a shrug. But it leaves a question: the person
reporting severe trouble with focus is not making it up, and their focus really
is worse than it should be on the objective task. So what *is* going on?

The rest of this document is about the four other explanations the same data
supports, and how strongly.

---

## The single most striking thing in the record

The person rated their own focus and organisation at **8 out of 100**. That is
about as low as the scale goes.

On the actual button-pressing test they:

- pressed the wrong button only **2.45%** of the time (allowed: 3%)
- missed a target only **8.57%** of the time (allowed: 10%)
- produced an unusually **strong** brain response to new information —
  17.3 microvolts where 6 is the floor

Impulse control tested fine. Sustained attention tested fine. The capacity to
notice something new is close to three times the minimum.

What *did* fail was **timing**:

- reaction time **588 ms**, where 500 is the ceiling
- responses **twice as uneven** as they should be (19 ms of jitter, limit 10)
- the first visual response of the brain arrives **38 ms late**

So the machinery is intact and running behind schedule. That is a very
different problem from the machinery being broken, and it points at completely
different things to try.

## Four stories the data supports, in order of strength

The analysis scored each story against what published research says should
happen, then checked whether the agreement could have come from chance. Higher
C means better agreement; a low p means chance is a poor explanation.

### 1. Low mood is driving how bad the thinking feels  — C = +0.74

The strongest pattern in the whole record, by a distance.

The depression questionnaire scored 12 (moderate), the anxiety one 13
(moderate), and the PTSD checklist 49 — one point under the usual cut-off, but
still far above normal. Self-rated mood came in at 17 out of 100.

There is a large literature showing that when mood is low, people rate their
own thinking as much worse than it tests. That is exactly the shape of this
record: the *feeling* of not being able to focus is severe and real, and it is
tracking mood more closely than it is tracking performance.

The vendor report flags this too and recommends a risk assessment. That is the
one item on this page that is time-sensitive.

### 2. The body's stress system is running hot  — C = +0.57

Overall heart-rate variability looks fine — in fact it looks good. But *how*
that variability is distributed does not.

Healthy heart rhythm spreads its variation across three frequency bands. Here
the slowest band, the one associated with stress load and slow recovery, is the
largest, and the fastest band — the "brake" that the calming half of the nervous
system controls — is only 11% of the total when it should be at least 15%.

The brainwave data agrees: two bands over the top of the head are elevated in a
pattern described as a "readiness" state. Useful in the middle of a task.
Unhelpful when you are sitting still with your eyes closed.

### 3. An immune system running hot  — C = +0.53

**26 of the 58 allergens tested came back positive.** Tree pollens especially:
black walnut, shagbark hickory and eastern oak all produced 15 mm reactions,
five times the threshold. Timothy grass, 15 mm. Dust mite, 11 mm. Cat, 7 mm.

Two things follow.

First, the test was done on **18 August, in New York** — the middle of ragweed
and mugwort season. So the person was probably symptomatic on the day their
brain was measured.

Second, and this is the important one: **the medication field on the report says
UNKNOWN.** Antihistamines slow reaction time. So does untreated hay fever. The
two headline abnormal findings — slow reaction time and a late first visual
response — are exactly the findings an antihistamine would produce.

Nobody wrote down whether one had been taken.

### 4. Everything is running late rather than running badly  — C = +0.40

Covered above. Amplitude fine everywhere, arrival time late everywhere. This is
the profile of fatigue and stress cost, not of a missing capability.

### And the one that did not hold up: textbook ADHD  — C = +0.12, p = 0.17

Scored the same way as the other four, using the same published relationships,
and it came out indistinguishable from randomly reshuffling the same numbers.

This is an independent check that lands in the same place as the vendor's own
screen, which is worth something.

## The flags that are not really flags

Three findings were marked abnormal on the vendor report and are, on inspection,
hairline:

| Finding | Result | Limit | Over by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theta:Beta ratio (the classic ADHD number) | 2.190 | 2.1 | **4%** |
| Peak alpha frequency | 11.10 Hz | 11.0 Hz | **0.9%** |
| P300a latency (attention speed) | 468 ms | 450 ms | **4%** |

And the theta:beta number carries a second problem the report states itself: the
reference tables it is compared against **do not extend past age 31**, and this
person is 31 years and 2 months old.

Compare those to what is genuinely out:

| Finding | Over by |
|---|---|
| Anxiety questionnaire | +225% |
| Depression questionnaire | +200% |
| Share of allergy panel positive | +199% |
| Slow-band heart rhythm dominance | +135% |
| Self-rated executive function | +86% |
| Response jitter | +86% |

A red dot on a report tells you a line was crossed. It does not tell you by how
much. Getting that distance onto the page is most of what this project does.

## The thing nobody printed

The report says, in prose, on the ERP page, that the P300b response should be at
least **50%** as strong as the P300a.

It is **38%** — 6.6 microvolts against 17.3.

The report never divides those two numbers anywhere in seventeen pages.

In plain terms: novelty grabs this person's attention hard, and the follow-
through that turns "something happened" into "and here is what it was" is
comparatively quiet. That is a fair description of what the person actually
complained about, and it fits far better than the theta:beta number that got
flagged instead.

## Four things that limit all of the above

1. **Four of nineteen brainwave sensors were unusable** (FZ, T8, P3, P8), so the
   software refused to run its brain-location analysis at all. The unusable set
   includes the front-middle sensor, which is the one most relevant to the
   attention claims. Any statement about *where* in the brain something is
   happening is weaker than it looks.

2. **Medications: UNKNOWN**, on a day when the person was probably taking an
   antihistamine.

3. **The heart-rhythm analysis used a 2-minute recording.** The slow band that
   drives finding #2 really wants about five minutes to estimate properly. The
   direction is probably right; the exact number is soft.

4. **The 12-lead heart tracing was never signed by a doctor.** The machine read
   it as a normal variant — a common, benign pattern in young men — and the
   intervals are all normal. But the comment field literally says "Unconfirmed
   Report". That is an administrative loose end, not a cardiac one.

## Three questions worth taking to an appointment

1. Can the cognitive block be repeated **off antihistamine and out of pollen
   season**? It is cheap, and it would settle whether the two headline
   abnormalities are neurology or pharmacology.

2. If mood and sleep are treated, **do the reaction-time numbers move**? If the
   strongest pattern in the record is real, they should.

3. Can someone **sign off the 12-lead**?

## How to look at the data yourself

```
make            # build everything
make dash       # then open build/dashboard.html in any browser
make explore    # or the Tk explorer, if you have a display
```

Hover anything. Click to pin it. Keys `1`–`5` turn each of the five stories on
as an overlay, and they combine. Keys `p`, `f`, `b` switch between plain
language, the technical version, and both at once — this document and the next
one are the same two registers, in the same words.
