A history in six circuits

The Current and the Word

New York City municipal electricity rights, the Rural Electrification Administration, and the long political project of turning light — like literacy — from a guarded privilege of the few into a utility that flows like water. And what happens when we assume the job is finished.

Circuit IThe Scribal Age of Power

Every civilization keeps certain technologies behind a velvet rope before it lets everyone touch them. For most of recorded history, the rope was around writing itself. Literacy belonged to scribes, priests, notaries, and the merchants who could pay them — a professional caste whose monopoly on the written word was also a monopoly on contracts, scripture, law, and memory. To read was to rule. The medieval scriptorium was not a library; it was a vault.[1]

Electricity arrived in New York the same way: as an aristocratic craft skill, performed for the wealthy by a priesthood of engineers. In December 1880, Charles Brush's blinding arc lamps went up along Broadway between 14th and 26th Streets — the origin of the "Great White Way" — as a public spectacle paid for by merchants who wanted their shop windows glowing.[2] Two years later, on September 4, 1882, Thomas Edison threw the switch at Pearl Street Station at 255–257 Pearl Street in lower Manhattan — the first commercial central power station in the United States. Its first district covered roughly one square mile of the Financial District. Its first customers numbered 82, lighting about 400 lamps; among the earliest places lit were the offices of the New York Times and the bank of Drexel, Morgan & Co.[3]

Note who got the current first: not tenements, not schools — banks and newspapers. J. P. Morgan, Edison's financier, had his Madison Avenue mansion wired as one of the first electrically lit private residences in the city, with its own basement generator that terrified the neighbors and occasionally set the desk on fire.[4] Electric light in 1882 was what an illuminated manuscript was in 1382: a bespoke luxury demonstrating that its owner commanded scarce, learned labor. The parallel is nearly exact — a technology of enormous general utility, introduced as an ornament of private wealth, its expertise guarded by the few who could conjure it.

The city's streets in those years were a physical diagram of unregulated franchise capitalism: dozens of competing lighting and telegraph companies strung so many wires overhead that the sky above lower Broadway looked caged. It took the Great Blizzard of 1888 — which tore the wire-jungle down and killed linemen and pedestrians — to force the wires underground and to establish the first real principle of municipal electricity rights in New York: the city, not the companies, owns the streets.[5] Every later fight over public power grows from that seed: the corporation's wires cross the public's right of way, and therefore the public has standing to set terms.

Circuit IIThe Gutenberg Moment

Literacy's rope was cut by a machine. Gutenberg's press (c. 1440s) did not teach anyone to read, but it collapsed the price of the written word so far that mass literacy became imaginable, then expected, then — centuries later, with compulsory public schooling — a civic right.[6] Electricity's Gutenberg moment was alternating current. Westinghouse and Tesla's AC system, proven at scale when Niagara Falls power reached Buffalo in 1896, meant current could travel far from the dynamo — power no longer had to live within a square mile of its station.[7]

And just as the press first filled public spaces with print — broadsides, church bibles, town notices — before most households owned books, electricity in New York became common in public before it became common at home. The sequence matters. Street lighting came first; then the workplace and the office tower; then the subway — the IRT opened in 1904 powered by its own colossal generating plant on 59th Street, arguably the city's first great public-purpose power station; then schools, theaters, and department stores.[8] A New Yorker of 1910 might work, ride, shop, and study under electric light all day and still go home to gaslight or kerosene. Electricity was a common luxury — experienced collectively, owned privately, priced monopolistically.

The private owners consolidated relentlessly. The dozens of Edison-era franchise companies merged into New York Edison, Brooklyn Edison, and their siblings under the Consolidated Gas Company, which in 1936 renamed itself Consolidated Edison — the investor-owned monopoly that has run the city's wires ever since.[9] By the 1920s the pattern was set nationally: electricity was urban, corporate, and profitable — and it stopped dead at the city limits, exactly where the profit did.

Circuit IIIThe Fight for Municipal Electricity Rights in New York

"Municipal electricity rights" names a question New York has been arguing about for 120 years: does the public merely regulate the current, or may it own it?

The argument became mass politics early. In 1905, newspaper magnate William Randolph Hearst ran for mayor of New York at the head of the Municipal Ownership League, on a platform of city-owned gas, electricity, and transit; he lost by a few thousand votes amid credible accusations that Tammany Hall stole the count. Municipal ownership of utilities was not a fringe idea — it nearly won City Hall.[10] Progressive-era reformers pressed the same case statewide: Governor Al Smith spent the 1920s vetoing giveaways of state water-power sites to private utilities, insisting the rivers were public property.[11]

The decisive victory came under Governor Franklin D. Roosevelt. In 1931 he signed the Power Authority Act, creating the Power Authority of the State of New York (today NYPA) to develop the St. Lawrence River's hydropower as a public trust — with the explicit purpose of using publicly generated power as a "yardstick" to measure and discipline private utility rates. Roosevelt's principle was blunt: the water power of the state belongs to the people, and its benefits should reach them as cheap electricity, not as dividends.[12]

The water power of the State should belong to all the people… It is the duty of the legislative bodies to see that this power… is transformed into usable electrical energy and distributed at the lowest possible cost. — Franklin D. Roosevelt, as Governor of New York[12]

NYPA became — and remains — the largest state public power organization in the country. Its massive St. Lawrence and Niagara hydro projects were built in the 1950s under Robert Moses, and its current flows today through the most municipal veins New York City has: NYPA supplies the electricity for the subway and commuter rails, the public schools, the public hospitals, public housing, street lights, and hundreds of other government facilities in the five boroughs.[13] New York City never municipalized Con Edison — campaigns to do so have flared repeatedly, most recently after the July 2019 Manhattan blackout, when elected officials from the City Council to Congress openly proposed a public takeover — but the city has always kept one foot planted in public power.[14] Beyond the city, dozens of New York municipalities — Jamestown, Plattsburgh, Fairport, Massena and others — run their own municipal electric systems, typically with rates far below the investor-owned utilities, a standing demonstration of the yardstick working.[15]

What "municipal electricity rights" has meant in practice

1888: The city asserts ownership of the streets; wires go underground on public terms. 1905: Municipal ownership nearly wins the mayoralty. 1931: New York State creates NYPA — public generation as a public trust. 1930s–present: NYPA powers the city's subways, schools, housing, and street lights. 2019: Blackouts revive the Con Ed municipalization debate. 2023: The Build Public Renewables Act orders NYPA to build publicly owned renewable generation.

Circuit IVThe REA: Mass Literacy for Power

If Pearl Street was electricity's scribal age and the urban grid its Gutenberg moment, the Rural Electrification Administration was its compulsory-schooling movement — the deliberate political decision that everyone would be brought inside, because markets alone never would.

The market's verdict on rural America had been in for fifty years: not profitable. In the mid-1930s, roughly nine in ten American farms had no electric service. Private utilities refused to build lines into the countryside or demanded that farmers pay the construction costs themselves. Farm families hauled water by hand, lit kerosene lamps, and watched town glow on the horizon.[16]

President Roosevelt — carrying his New York public-power convictions to Washington — created the REA by Executive Order 7037 on May 11, 1935; Congress made it permanent with the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 (the Norris–Rayburn Act). The mechanism was radically simple: the federal government lent money at low interest not primarily to utilities but to farmer-owned cooperatives — neighbors incorporating to string their own lines. Private utilities fought back with "spite lines" built to skim the densest customers and break co-op territories, and lobbied against the program as socialism; the co-ops built anyway. By 1953, more than 90 percent of American farms had electricity. It stands among the most successful infrastructure programs in American history.[16]

The REA's deepest lesson is the one Maggie Appleton draws, in "Home-Cooked Software and Barefoot Developers," from a different mass campaign: revolutionary China's barefoot doctors. Facing a countryside with almost no physicians, China trained over a million villagers — farmers with a few months of medical education — to deliver basic care where credentialed medicine would never go. They were not lesser doctors; they were a different shape of doctor, native to the places they served.[17] The REA lineman and the barefoot doctor are the same figure: the neighbor-expert, produced deliberately and at scale, because waiting for the professional market to reach the periphery means waiting forever. Appleton's argument is that software now needs its barefoot practitioners; this essay's argument is that electricity found its own in the 1930s, and has since forgotten how it did it.

Circuit VThe Blip: When the Curve Bends Back

Here is where the story stops being a victory lap.

The comfortable narrative — for literacy and for electricity alike — is a smooth ascending curve: privilege, then diffusion, then universality, then permanence. The data no longer cooperates. Global adult literacy rates still inch upward on paper (roughly 86% to 88% over the last decade), but the UN's own accounting shows the project stalling and locally reversing: about 739–754 million adults remain unable to read, nearly two-thirds of them women, and in sub-Saharan Africa the absolute number of illiterate adults has been rising — from roughly 196 million toward 225 million — as population growth outruns schooling. Post-pandemic, UNESCO and the World Bank estimated "learning poverty" — ten-year-olds unable to read a simple sentence — at around 70 percent of children in low- and middle-income countries.[18] The rate can rise while the number of people left outside grows. That is what stalling out looks like from inside the statistics.

Electricity's curve bent back at the same moment, and this time it isn't even ambiguous. In 2022, for the first time in decades, the number of human beings without electricity access increased — to about 685 million people, 10 million more than the year before, over 80 percent of them in sub-Saharan Africa, where some 570 million people live without power. The IEA and World Bank now describe global electrification as stagnating, with the 2030 universal-access goal receding rather than approaching.[19]

And in the war zones the proxies fall in pairs, because they were never independent — each one holds the others up. Where the grid collapses — Gaza, Yemen, Syria, the targeted Ukrainian power system — water pumping and sewage treatment fail with it, then vaccine refrigeration, then hospitals, then schools; cholera and measles return to places that had beaten them, and a generation misses the classroom years in which literacy is cheap to acquire and after which it is expensive.[20] The "taken-for-granted" package of first-world life — light that flows like water, water that flows at all, reading as a birthright, childhood illness as an inconvenience — turns out to be a single braided cable. Cut it anywhere and every strand frays.

Universality is not a ratchet. It is maintenance — political maintenance — and it can be deferred until it fails.

New York City itself keeps a folk memory of this fragility: the 1965 Northeast blackout, the 1977 "Night of Terror" blackout — twenty-five hours of darkness, looting, and a thousand fires that showed how thin the membrane is between a wired civilization and none — and the 2003 cascade.[21] The lights came back each time because institutions existed whose job was to bring them back. That is the entire difference between a blip and a collapse: not the outage, but the institution standing behind it.

Circuit VIThe Next Electrification

Which returns the story to municipal electricity rights, because New York has just written the newest chapter. In 2023, after a four-year organizing campaign by the Public Power NY coalition, the state passed the Build Public Renewables Act — directing NYPA, the very institution FDR chartered in 1931, to plan, build, own, and operate renewable generation at scale, with bill credits reserved for low-income customers in disadvantaged communities and union labor guaranteed on every project.[22] It is the REA's logic, refiled under a new docket: where decarbonized power is not being built fast enough or fairly enough by the market, the public builds it.

The lesson of the whole arc — scriptorium to Pearl Street, Gutenberg to the AC grid, compulsory schooling to the REA, barefoot doctors to co-op linemen — is that the last mile of any civilizational technology is never reached by diffusion alone. It is reached by a decision, usually a public one, usually against the advice of the incumbents, to treat the thing as a right and to mass-produce the neighbor-experts who can deliver it. Literacy needed the public school. Rural America needed the co-op. The unlit two-thirds of a billion people need their own REA — solar mini-grid cooperatives, barefoot electricians, public authorities with a mandate — and the already-lit world needs to remember that its own light is an institution, not a law of nature.

The rope is always waiting to be re-hung. The history of New York's electricity is the history of people repeatedly deciding to cut it.
