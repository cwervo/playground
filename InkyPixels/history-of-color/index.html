A History of Photographic Color

Cyanotypes, daguerreotypes, tin etching, painted black-and-white photography, the invention of colored-crystal photochemistry, and the long story of film grain and film-stock size (16 mm → 70 mm) that gave us more and more and more development. 1842 · The Cyanotype

iron salts · Prussian blue

Anna Atkins presses ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide into a single, stubborn blue. There is only one channel worth speaking of — cyan — so the C-well floods while the others sit nearly dry. A photograph that is also a chemistry lesson in what a single ink can carry.

1839 · The Daguerreotype

silvered copper · a mirror with a memory

A polished silver plate, fumed with iodine and mercury, holds an image so fine it must be tilted into the dark to be seen at all. No pigment, only tone — the K-well dominates as pure luminance, the mirror doing the work of ink.

1850s · Tin Etching & the Tintype

japanned iron · the field portrait

Collodion on a lacquered iron plate: cheap, immediate, tough enough to mail home from the front. The palette warms — blacks and a scorched sepia — so K and Y climb together while cyan barely wets the nib.

1860s · Painting the Photograph

hand-tint · applied color before color existed

Before the chemistry could hold a hue, colorists brushed it on — a blush of magenta at the cheek, a wash of yellow at the lamp. The wells show it: a monochrome base under deliberate, local M and Y.

1907 · Colored-Crystal Photochemistry

autochrome · dyed potato-starch grains

The Lumière brothers scatter microscopic grains of starch, dyed orange, green and violet, across the plate — a random color mosaic that filters light before it ever reaches the silver. The first time all four wells fill at once: a true, if pointillist, color.

1930s · The Story of Film Grain

emulsion · the texture of light

Silver-halide crystals are the grain, and the grain is the image. Push the stock and the crystals bloom; the whole frame breathes with noise. Coverage rides high across C M Y with a restless K underneath.

16mm → 70mm · More and More Development

stock size · resolution as real estate

A bigger negative is simply more silver to develop — finer grain, deeper color, more room for every ink to sit down cleanly. From 16 mm newsreels to 70 mm spectacle, each jump in stock size is another jump in how much color the page can honestly hold.
